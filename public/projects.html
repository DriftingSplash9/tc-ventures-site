The Economic Report Influence Graph

Every important number in public life — inflation, a disability payment, how much your city gets for road repair — is calculated from some other number, which was calculated from another, back to a handful of foundational statistical releases. Almost nobody can see that structure, including the people who depend on it.

This draws it. Each sphere is a real published report, each line a documented dependency between two of them, and a report’s size is how much everything else rests on it. It runs in a browser, in 3D, with no backend.

The rule the whole project runs on

No document, no edge. A line is drawn only if a published document says, in words I can quote, that one report uses the other as an input. Leads I cannot source go to a dropped list with a reason attached. Every existing line is graded A, B or C on how well its cited document actually supports it, and the grade is visible in the app, because a map that hides its own weak spots is worse than no map.

One city’s budget, traced all the way back

This is the slice that sits above my own city. Start at the City of Grande Prairie’s budget report, follow only what it is documented to depend on, and keep following: fifty-odd published reports and seventy-odd dependencies, ending at statistical standards written by the United Nations. The road-repair money is in there.

The still is a render of the same data. Nothing three-dimensional is downloaded until you ask for it.

One path through it, in words

Six documents, and every link graded on how well the document I cited actually supports it. Two of these are C’s. They stay visible, and they stay graded C, because the weak joins are the ones a reader most needs to see.

  1. Uses data from · graded B City Budget Report, City of Grande Prairie → Local Government Fiscal Framework — Capital Funding Allocations, Alberta Municipal Affairs
  2. Is calculated from · graded C Local Government Fiscal Framework → Alberta Budget — Fiscal Plan, Alberta Treasury Board and Finance
  3. Uses data from · graded A Alberta Budget — Fiscal Plan → Canada Health Transfer, Department of Finance Canada
  4. Is calculated from · graded C Canada Health Transfer → National Economic Accounts (Quarterly), Statistics Canada
  5. Follows the methodology of · graded B National Economic Accounts → System of National Accounts 2008, United Nations, European Commission, IMF, OECD and World Bank

What is actually hard about it

Not the 3D. The hard part is holding one standard across eighty-odd research sessions without drift, which is why the repository carries a fixed extraction protocol, two lane playbooks and exactly one handoff file rather than a pile of notes — and why a validator runs on every data change and refuses to pass on a dangling reference.

The other hard part is drawing a corpus this size without lying. Cluster spacing, node size scaling and camera framing all change what a viewer believes about the data, so each is measured rather than eyeballed.

It is not finished, by design

The corpus grows. Statistical agencies publish on their own schedules, so the graph is built to keep looking — new releases get pulled in, new dependencies get sourced and graded, and the counts move every week. Any exact figure quoted here would be wrong by the time you read it, which is why you will not find one.

Rocket Lander

Land a rocket, gently, with the fuel you were given. It falls belly-down through layered, gusting wind, flips, burns, and has to sit down on a droneship that is bobbing on swell and drifting away from you. A run lasts twenty to sixty seconds. Failure is instant and obviously your own fault.

The teaching idea came first: the tuning knobs are the interface, so a nine-to-twelve-year-old learns the physics by flying against it rather than reading about it. There are 38 of them behind one key — gravity, thrust, gimbal authority, drag, wind, fuel, pad tolerances — and the panel builds itself from a spec table, so adding a knob is one variable and one row.

Three things the build taught me

Thrust from the tail does nothing on its own. A nozzle pointing down the body axis pushes straight through the centre of mass and produces no torque — which is exactly why a real rocket’s engine being at the bottom does not tip it over. Gimballing the nozzle is what makes it steer.

Drag had to be split in two. Along-hull and across-hull flow are dragged separately, and across is about twelve times along. That split is the entire reason the belly-flop is worth doing: tilting off broadside produces lift, and that glide is the only way to reach a barge that is not directly beneath you.

A restoring force without a damper is a pendulum. The weathervane moment that settles the ship belly-down is a spring; with no aerodynamic damper against it, the ship swings forever. Easy to forget, extremely obvious the moment it is missing.

Where it is

It flies. Landing it is brutally hard — which is the direction, not the bug: real physics and real logic, difficult but winnable, the way the actual thing is difficult but winnable. What is left is tuning and time.

And three live sites

Written and maintained solo, and deliberately three different designs rather than one template reused. Bare Your Rare is a patient-led site for ultra-rare conditions, built because most people searching for one of them find almost nothing. The Grande Prairie Residential Society site serves a volunteer-run nonprofit providing barrier-free housing — 87 units before the Margaret Edgson Manor fire, with the rebuild under way and the final count not yet settled. Accessibility and plain language are first-order requirements there, not decoration. thomascheesman.ca is the personal one, where the experiments and the things I build for my kids live.
