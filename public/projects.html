In active development · TypeScript, React, Three.js

The Economic Report Influence Graph

Every important number in public life — inflation, a disability payment, how much your city gets for road repair — is calculated from some other number, which was calculated from another, back to a handful of foundational statistical releases. Almost nobody can see that structure, including the people who depend on it.

This draws it. Each sphere is a real published report, each line a documented dependency between two of them, and a report’s size is how much everything else rests on it. It runs in a browser, in 3D, with no backend.

The rule the whole project runs on

No document, no edge. A line is drawn only if a published document says, in words I can quote, that one report uses the other as an input. Leads I cannot source go to a dropped list with a reason attached. Every existing line is graded A, B or C on how well its cited document actually supports it, and the grade is visible in the app, because a map that hides its own weak spots is worse than no map.

One city’s budget, traced all the way back

This is the slice that sits above my own city. Start at the City of Grande Prairie’s budget report, follow only what it is documented to depend on, and keep following: fifty-odd published reports and seventy-odd dependencies, ending at statistical standards written by the United Nations. The road-repair money is in there.

The still is a render of the same data. Nothing three-dimensional is downloaded until you ask for it.

One path through it, in words

Six documents, and every link graded on how well the document I cited actually supports it. Two of these are C’s. They stay visible, and they stay graded C, because the weak joins are the ones a reader most needs to see.

  1. Uses data from · graded B City Budget Report, City of Grande Prairie → Local Government Fiscal Framework — Capital Funding Allocations, Alberta Municipal Affairs
  2. Is calculated from · graded C Local Government Fiscal Framework → Alberta Budget — Fiscal Plan, Alberta Treasury Board and Finance
  3. Uses data from · graded A Alberta Budget — Fiscal Plan → Canada Health Transfer, Department of Finance Canada
  4. Is calculated from · graded C Canada Health Transfer → National Economic Accounts (Quarterly), Statistics Canada
  5. Follows the methodology of · graded B National Economic Accounts → System of National Accounts 2008, United Nations, European Commission, IMF, OECD and World Bank

What is actually hard about it

Not the 3D. The hard part is holding one standard across eighty-odd research sessions without drift, which is why the repository carries a fixed extraction protocol, two lane playbooks and exactly one handoff file rather than a pile of notes — and why a validator runs on every data change and refuses to pass on a dangling reference.

The other hard part is drawing a corpus this size without lying. Cluster spacing, node size scaling and camera framing all change what a viewer believes about the data, so each is measured rather than eyeballed.

It is not finished, by design

The corpus grows. Statistical agencies publish on their own schedules, so the graph is built to keep looking — new releases get pulled in, new dependencies get sourced and graded, and the counts move every week. Any exact figure quoted here would be wrong by the time you read it, which is why you will not find one.

The Back Quarter

The homepage of my personal site is not a page. It is a quarter section of farmland you drive around in a buggy, and every building on it is a part of the site: the farmhouse is the about page, the grain elevator is the page about my diagnosis, the arcade barn boots the games, the church on the hill holds the family histories. Drive up to one and it opens.

I grew up on that land. We moved north to the Peace Country when I was ten, and the farms came one after another — a bird farm at Teepee Creek, LaGlace, a pig farm, then Little Smokey with no gas, a wood furnace and a rabbit trapline. The Back Quarter is that country rebuilt from memory and handed to a visitor as the way in. A menu would have done the job. This does the job and says something while it does it.

Three things the build taught me

A vehicle that can turn on the spot does not feel like a vehicle. Early on the buggy pivoted like a tank and nothing about it read as driving. The fix was not more physics, it was less permission: yaw rate now follows forward speed, so she has to be rolling before she will turn, and she eases into it. Grip drops in the wet. Reversing flips the steering sense, the way it does in a real yard.

Bloom is a night instrument. Glow is driven by the same day factor that runs the sky, so it climbs after sunset and falls away at dawn. I learned that the hard way: for one version the daylight walls glowed too, and the whole farm looked like moulded plastic. Light has to behave like light, or the painting stops being a place.

The fire sounded like someone bashing a tin can. Every pop was the same noise through the same filter, so every pop rang the same note, and an ear catches that instantly. It is now a low ember roar with discrete pops a few hundredths of a second long, each one through its own randomly tuned filter. Nothing rings. Same amount of code, completely different room.

The rule underneath it

A homepage has to stay a homepage. Nothing heavy loads until the visitor asks for it — the engines sit behind a click — the painted farm is a real image before any of it boots, and every destination in the world is also an ordinary link in the ordinary menu. If the graphics fail, or a visitor never touches them, the site still works exactly as a site should. A world you can drive around should be the reward for wanting one, never the toll gate on the way in.

The Desk and the Drawer

The same site's navigation is a photograph of the desk I work at. The objects on it are the menu: the monitor holds the table of contents, the keyboard opens site search, a toad on the desk opens an arcade, a bin of memory cards opens a slideshow, the mouse changes the cursor trail. Hover anything and it tells you what it is. The footer is the other half — a drawer that slides open as you reach the bottom, with the sitemap in it, a clock running on my time, my email stored rot13 so scrapers get nonsense, and a marble that starts a pinball table.

None of that is the interesting part. The interesting part is that it costs a visitor nothing to ignore.

Three things the build taught me

An interface that is a picture still has to be a menu. There is a plain-list toggle on the desk, a separate ordinary navigation for phones, a full keyboard path through every hotspot, and an Escape key that closes exactly one layer at a time — drawer, then search, then the overlay itself. A visitor who wants a list of links gets a list of links, one click in. The picture is the reward, never the toll.

Nothing loads until someone asks for it. The physics engine and the renderer behind the pinball table are most of a megabyte between them, and neither one touches the network until a visitor clicks the marble. Same for the arcade, the slideshow and the desk art itself. A visitor who never clicks pays nothing for any of it, which is the only honest way to put a game in a footer.

Plan for the dependency that does not arrive. The pinball table wants a WebGL renderer, but if that script fails to load it does not fail with it: it notices the missing library and boots its plain-canvas renderer instead. The game always starts. That habit came out of kitchens, where the delivery not showing up is not an excuse, it is Tuesday.

And four live sites

Written and maintained solo, and deliberately four different designs rather than one template reused.

Bare Your Rare is a patient-led site for ultra-rare conditions, built because most people searching for one of them find almost nothing. It now carries five guides written for patients rather than clinicians — Hajdu-Cheney syndrome, Erdheim-Chester disease, Fechtner syndrome, POEMS syndrome and stiff person syndrome — each one a long read assembled from the literature and put into plain language.

The Grande Prairie Residential Society is a volunteer-run nonprofit providing barrier-free housing — 87 units before the Margaret Edgson Manor fire, with the rebuild under way and the final count not yet settled. I sit on the board, and the site is my donation to it: built, hosted and maintained at no cost to the society. Accessibility and plain language are first-order requirements there, not decoration. Read the case study.

thomascheesman.ca is the personal one, and the place I try things that have no business on a client site — The Back Quarter is its front door and The Desk is its menu, both written up above. The fourth is this one, the only one of the four with no WordPress under it: hand-written, no framework, no build step. Read the case study.
