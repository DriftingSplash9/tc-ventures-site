Rocket Lander

A rocket with a real aerodynamic flight model, running in your browser. It flies. I cannot land it yet, and neither will you.

Land a rocket, gently, with the fuel you were given. It falls belly-down through layered, gusting wind, flips, burns, and has to sit down on a droneship that is bobbing on swell and drifting away from you. A run lasts twenty to sixty seconds. Failure is instant and obviously your own fault.

This is on the site unfinished on purpose. The flight model is the work; the game around it is not built yet, and the landing is the part I have not solved. What is worth looking at is what the rocket does in the air, and the panel you can open to argue with it.

Fly it

The build is about thirty megabytes, so it downloads only when you ask for it. Click the canvas once after it loads — the keys do not reach the game until it has focus.

Two control systems share one set of keys. The flaps only bite where there is air moving past them; the gimbal only steers while the engines are lit. W/S is pitch in both. A/D banks you on the flaps and yaws you on the gimbal — the same intent on different axes, because one gimbal physically cannot roll a rocket about its own long axis. There is no mode switch; which system answers you depends on where you are in the descent.

The panel is the point

The teaching idea came first: the tuning knobs are the interface, so a nine-to-twelve-year-old learns the physics by flying against it rather than reading about it. There are 38 of them behind one key — gravity, thrust, gimbal authority, drag, wind, fuel, pad tolerances — and the panel builds itself from a spec table, so adding a knob is one variable and one row.

Turn the gravity down and you will land it on your first go. That is allowed. The knobs are there so you can find out which one was making it hard, which is the whole lesson.

Three things the build taught me

Thrust from the tail does nothing on its own. A nozzle pointing down the body axis pushes straight through the centre of mass and produces no torque — which is exactly why a real rocket’s engine being at the bottom does not tip it over. Gimballing the nozzle is what makes it steer.

Drag had to be split in two. Along-hull and across-hull flow are dragged separately, and across is about twelve times along. That split is the entire reason the belly-flop is worth doing: tilting off broadside produces lift, and that glide is the only way to reach a barge that is not directly beneath you.

A restoring force without a damper is a pendulum. The weathervane moment that settles the ship belly-down is a spring; with no aerodynamic damper against it, the ship swings forever. Easy to forget, extremely obvious the moment it is missing.

Where it actually is

It flies, and it flies the way the maths said it would. Landing it is brutally hard — which is the direction, not the bug: real physics and real logic, difficult but winnable, the way the actual thing is difficult but winnable. Whether it is winnable by me, on these numbers, is still open. That is what the tuning is for, and tuning is what is left.

The scoring, the levels and the menu are not built. I would rather put the honest middle of a project on a website than wait until there is nothing left to show.
